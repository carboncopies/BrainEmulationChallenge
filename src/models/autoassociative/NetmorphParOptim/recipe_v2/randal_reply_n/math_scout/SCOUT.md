## Ranking: top 5 for us (details and references follow)
1. **Offline weight design by perceptron-style / convex programming under sign and bound constraints** (Problem 1). Lowest cost, targets the most binding limit (our "every pair strong" rule), and tells us the real capacity bound for the grown graph.
2. **Prune the touch-detected synapses to a target connectome instead of tuning D_synmax** (Problems 1 and 4; also answers Randal's "weight 0" question). Offline, and it is what the striatum model (Snudda) does.
3. **Inhibition design from balance and threshold-linear theory, with an inhibition-stabilized-network (ISN) test** (Problems 1 and 3). Treat GABA strength and timing as a constraint to be solved, not tuned.
4. **Homeostatic normalisation between recall epochs** (Problem 2): synaptic scaling plus heterosynaptic competition, emulated by re-setting weights outside the simulator.
5. **Surrogate model / simulation-based inference over the growth parameters, fitted on our existing growth runs** (Problem 4), with CMA-ES or Bayesian optimisation as the cheaper fallback.
Not in the top 5 but worth a note: theta/gamma pacing of retrieval (Problem 3) is the right biology for Randal's "theta mode" request, but needs time-varying input or a NES feature; it is item 6.

## Problem 1: tiny capacity, cross-triggering overlaps, summation on shared targets

### 1a. Willshaw / sparse associative memory and its capacity
- Summary: binary-synapse (Willshaw-type) memories store about 0.69 bits per synapse when patterns are very sparse (active units k about log n) and fall toward zero when k is much larger than log n (secondary source in the search). With incomplete connectivity, information efficiency can be kept high with very low connectivity (about 10% in Graham and Willshaw) provided the firing thresholds are set properly (Buckingham and Willshaw); structural plasticity (pruning) is analysed by Knoblauch, Palm and Sommer.
- Mapping: our memories have k = 4-8 active cells in n = 96 (ln 96 is about 4.6, **my arithmetic**), so they sit in the sparse regime these results address. The binding limit for us is not n or k but that a memory must be a set of pairs that are all strong, i.e. a nearly complete sub-graph of a graph with probability 0.28; classical capacity results assume weights learned over the whole matrix, not a graph restricted to strong pairs.
- Cost: offline. Compute what a Willshaw-style rule would store on the actual E4 graph, as a reference line against our packing result.
- References:

### 1b. Gardner capacity, optimal-stability learning, perceptron-style weight design
- Summary: Gardner (1988) gives the maximal number of random patterns a network can store over all learning rules; Krauth and Mezard (1987) give iterative rules that reach optimal stability. Brunel et al. (2004) apply the perceptron bound with the sign constraint (all weights excitatory) and show that the optimal weight distribution has more than 50% silent synapses; Brunel (2016) and Chapeton et al. (2012) apply the same idea to recurrent cortical networks. Davey and Adams report that sign-constrained Hopfield-type networks lose about half the capacity relative to unconstrained ones (the theoretical maximum is half), with smaller basins of attraction and longer training. A three-threshold learning rule is reported to approach the maximal capacity of recurrent networks while respecting Dale's principle.
- Mapping: the problem "choose per-pair weights in [0.1, 1.0], all excitatory, so that cueing 4 cells fires the other members of each memory and nothing else" is a convex feasibility problem (a linear program) per target cell, with margin variable kappa. Overlap cross-talk and "summation on shared targets" appear as explicit inequality constraints (non-member targets must stay below threshold when any other memory's cue is on). Only existing pairs can carry weight, so the graph sets the bound; the optimiser tells us how many memories the graph supports with distributed weights instead of our rule "every pair at least 4.125 nS". Silent synapses (weight at the lower bound) fit with Randal's suggestion of weight 0 for weak synapses; note the allowed range starts at 0.1, not 0.
- Cost: offline, a few hours of work (LP/QP solvers, margin sweep), then a short verification with the usual recall runs (cheap sims) once the weights can be set per pair. Assumes the API allows per-pair weights.
- Risks: the optimiser may find memories that fit on paper but fail dynamically (LIF timing, GABA); the bound is for static threshold units, not LIF. Weights of 0.1-1.0 may be too narrow a range to help.
- References:
  - Gardner E (1988) The space of interactions in neural network models. J Phys A 21:257-270. https://scilit.net/publications/ea10e2e0e9c5ba54a9357cf486515ca4 . VERIFIED.
  - Krauth W, Mezard M (1987) Learning algorithms with optimal stability in neural networks. J Phys A 20:L745-L752. https://www.marcmezard.fr/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/87_MK_JPA.pdf . VERIFIED.

### 1c. Balanced E/I memory networks and bounded weights
- Summary: Roudi and Latham (2007) show a balanced memory network whose capacity scales with the number of connections per neuron, not the number of neurons, and warn that with a fixed number of connections per neuron the capacity is capped whatever the network size; they suggest weakly coupled modules. Rubin, Abbott and Sompolinsky (2017) show that high capacity and noise-robust selectivity need strong, mostly cancelling excitation and inhibition and predict an optimal E/I synapse ratio. Fusi and Abbott (2007) show that bounded synapses reduce capacity drastically unless they have several states (for binary synapses capacity grows only logarithmically with synapse number).
- Mapping: a Pyr->Pyr in-degree of about 27 (96 cells x p 0.28, **my arithmetic**) puts an upper limit on how many memories a cell can take part in, independent of any recipe tweak; that is a theory-based reason to expect a small capacity and to test modules (several 96-cell patches) rather than more tuning inside one. Bounded weights (0.1-1.0) are the Fusi-Abbott regime, so we should expect capacity below the unbounded perceptron bound.
- Cost: offline for estimating the bound (few hours); modules would need larger grown networks (sim slots).
- References:
  - Roudi Y, Latham PE (2007) A balanced memory network. PLoS Comput Biol 3(9):e141. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1971123 . VERIFIED.
  - Rubin R, Abbott LF, Sompolinsky H (2017) Balanced excitation and inhibition are required for high-capacity, noise-robust neuronal selectivity. PNAS 114(44):E9366-E9375. doi 10.1073/pnas.1705841114 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5676886 . VERIFIED.
  - Fusi S, Abbott LF (2007) Limits on the memory storage capacity of bounded synapses. Nature Neuroscience 10:485-493. doi 10.1038/nn1859 ; https://www.nature.com/articles/nn1859 . VERIFIED (record only).

### 1d. k-winner-take-all / feedback inhibition in autoassociative models of CA3
- Summary: Treves and Rolls (1991) analyse autoassociative CA3 with threshold-linear units: capacity scales with the number of associatively modifiable recurrent connections per cell, and non-specific effects such as inhibition or neuromodulation change the overall response but not the capacity. Treves and Rolls (1992) argue that CA3 needs two input systems, one with strong non-modifiable synapses (they identify the perforant path-like input for cue and the mossy fibers for storage). Rennó-Costa et al. (2014) model CA3 as an attractor network and reproduce morphing experiments.
- Mapping: inhibition in these models is a global threshold that keeps the number of active cells at about k; in our grown nets inhibition is feed-forward and late (6 ms against 4.5 ms), so it cannot play that role. A cheap version is to compute offline what inhibitory weight and delay would hold the active count at k, using a threshold-linear approximation of E4, and compare with what the grown pairs deliver (0.57-0.82 nS per pair, `gaba_check_l`). The two-input idea matches Randal's example designs (strong, fixed cue pathway and plastic recurrent weights).
- Cost: offline for the estimate; cheap sims to check.
- References:
  - Treves A, Rolls ET (1991) What determines the capacity of autoassociative memories in the brain? Network 2(4):371-397. https://www.oxcns.org/papers/154_Treves+Rolls91.pdf . VERIFIED.
  - Treves A, Rolls ET (1992) Computational constraints suggest the need for two distinct input systems to the hippocampal CA3 network. Hippocampus 2:189-199. https://www.oxcns.org/papers/163_Treves+Rolls92.pdf . VERIFIED.

## Problem 2: STDP without depression or homeostasis

- Summary of options:
  - Synaptic scaling (Turrigiano 2008): all of a neuron's excitatory inputs are scaled so firing returns to baseline.
  - Heterosynaptic plasticity (Chistiakova et al. 2014): synapses that were not active are changed when the cell is strongly driven, counteracting Hebbian runaway.
  - Zenke, Agnes and Gerstner (2015): Hebbian plasticity plus rapid heterosynaptic plasticity, slower homeostasis and consolidation is sufficient for assemblies to form and be recalled by a partial cue, days later; removing any one component breaks stability.
  - Litwin-Kumar and Doiron (2014): assemblies form and are maintained in a spiking network through plasticity with homeostatic mechanisms (the sources disagree on which STDP rule was the main one).
  - Triplet STDP (Pfister and Gerstner 2006): rule using spike triplets that explains frequency dependence.
  - BCM (sliding threshold): recalled only (Bienenstock, Cooper and Munro 1982, J Neurosci; UNVERIFIED, not searched).
  - Inhibitory plasticity (Vogels et al. 2011, Hennequin et al. 2017): sets inhibition to balance excitation per cell.
  - CA3 reconsolidation / re-encoding at retrieval: **no source found** in my searches; Randal mentions recent results, but I have no reference.
- Mapping: the simulator STDP is AMPA-only and potentiation-only (A+ 0.027, A- 0.02, tau 7 ms, shift -4 ms as in `stdp_k`), with no hooks for scaling or heterosynaptic terms. Our finding (cue to non-cue pairs strengthen, selectivity lost after 10-100 recalls) is the classic runaway case these papers address. The practical route is an **outside-the-simulator** rule: run a recall epoch, read the STDP-changed weights, apply a multiplicative normalisation per target cell (sum of incoming weight held fixed, which is a scaling-plus-heterosynaptic update), write the weights back, run the next epoch. Within bounds 0.1-1.0 the normalisation also keeps weights legal. Triplet STDP or voltage-based rules would need a NES change.
- Cost: cheap sims (epoch-by-epoch loop) if weights can be read and written between runs; I did not check whether the API can do that. A first test can be done offline on the logged weight changes from `stdp_k` (180 sims already done): apply the normalisation to the recorded post-recall weights and see whether 3-cell selectivity returns (it is a replay of existing data, not new sims).
- References:
  - Zenke F, Agnes EJ, Gerstner W (2015) Diverse synaptic plasticity mechanisms orchestrated to form and retrieve memories in spiking neural networks. Nature Communications 6:6922. doi 10.1038/ncomms7922 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4411307 . VERIFIED.

  - Chistiakova M, Bannon NM, Bazhenov M, Volgushev M (2014) Heterosynaptic plasticity: multiple mechanisms and multiple roles. The Neuroscientist 20(5):483-498. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4924806 . VERIFIED.

## Problem 3: inhibition timing, feedback versus feed-forward, theta/gamma pacing

- Summary:
  - Inhibition-stabilized networks: Tsodyks et al. (1997) show that strong recurrent excitation that would be unstable without feedback inhibition gives a paradoxical response (extra drive to interneurons lowers their rate); Ozeki et al. (2009) use this to show that cat V1 is inhibition-stabilized; Miller and Palmigiano and later work generalise it.
  - Theta phases: Hasselmo, Bodelon and Wyble (2002) propose that each theta cycle separates an encoding phase (entorhinal input dominant) from a retrieval phase (CA3 input dominant), with depotentiation possible in the retrieval phase; Kunec, Hasselmo and Kopell (2005) is cited for the CA3 version. Lisman and Jensen (2013) describe items represented in successive gamma subcycles of a theta cycle; Colgin et al. (2009) report slow gamma in CA1 coherent with CA3 and fast gamma with entorhinal cortex.
  - Randal's reply of 22 Nov points at the same idea: lateral inhibition that spaces successive retrieved patterns, and silencing after a pattern fires so there is no reverberation.
- Mapping: (a) ISN test: if E4 recurrent excitation is strong enough to be ISN, raising drive to interneurons should lower their rate; this is a cheap diagnostic on the existing recall protocol, no new network (cheap sims). If E4 is not an ISN (weak recurrence, 0.28 probability, 2 synapses per pair), the late inhibition we see is expected and the lever is Pyr->Int strength and delay rather than more interneurons. (b) Theta pacing needs a periodic external drive or modulation of recurrent strength; whether NES supports time-varying input or modulation I did not check, so this is "needs NES change or time-varying stimulus". (c) The late feed-forward inhibition is also consistent with Randal's view that inhibition acts after the pattern fires; the open question is only whether it is strong enough to terminate reverberation (the c = 3.0 runaway result in headline_i says it is not).
- Cost: ISN test = cheap sims; theta pacing = needs time-varying drive (unknown) and a model design decision; I rank it 6th.
- References:
  - Tsodyks MV, Skaggs WE, Sejnowski TJ, McNaughton BL (1997) Paradoxical effects of external modulation of inhibitory interneurons. J Neurosci 17(11):4382-4388. VERIFIED (note: a search I wrote wrongly named Ermentrout as an author; the record lists the four above).

## Problem 4: connectivity from morphology, hitting target statistics, seed non-determinism

- Summary:
  - Touch detection plus pruning: Hjorth et al. (2020) build a mouse striatum model by detecting close axon-dendrite appositions ("touches") in reconstructed morphologies and then pruning putative synapses to match pairwise recorded connectivity; the software is Snudda.
  - Simulation-based inference: Gonçalves et al. (2020) train a neural density estimator on simulations (SNPE) to get posteriors over mechanistic-model parameters that match observed summary statistics; the sbi toolkit implements it.
  - CMA-ES (Hansen 2016 tutorial) is a standard derivative-free optimiser for noisy non-convex objectives; Bayesian optimisation (Snoek et al. 2012, recalled) is the alternative for expensive objectives.
  - Seed non-determinism: I found no literature specific to NetMorph; Randal says he is investigating it himself and that we may wait. A general technique, common random numbers, reduces variance when comparing settings, but needs repeatable random streams, which is the thing that is broken, so it is not usable now.
- Mapping:
  - Touch-detection pruning matches what NetMorph's D_synmax step is already (touch detection with a distance criterion) but adds an explicit second step: keep touches until Pyr->Pyr probability and synapses per pair hit the targets. We have the synapse lists, so we can do it offline on grown networks and then ask Randal whether the pruned synapses can be expressed as weight 0 or must be dropped. That removes the need to retune D_synmax and the ~50% pass rate for the density bars.
  - SBI/CMA-ES: our observed pass rate (5 of 10) is mainly caused by bar means close to the bounds (p 0.284 vs 0.3; Pyr->Pyr 2.05 vs 1.9; Int->Pyr 4.30 vs 4). A fitted surrogate over D_pyr, D_int, B_inf, number of interneurons, using the 60-odd existing growth runs, could move the recipe to the centre of the bands. The noise is large (regrowth with the same seed matches only about 70% of nodes; one run is 20-30 min), so the surrogate must model noise; with many sims already in hand the first step is offline regression, not new sims.
- Cost: pruning = offline, low; surrogate fit = offline first, then a few growths when slots free; SBI proper = hundreds of growths (not feasible now).
