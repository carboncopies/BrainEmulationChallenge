## 0. Reading of the bottleneck after this round
Two different things were called "the bottleneck" and the literature separates them:
1. **The grown network is not unusual in synapses per pair.** Our median of 1 synapse per pair and a mean of about 1.9 sit with the published values (1.85 and 4.5 quoted by van Ooyen 2014 and in `lit_review/LIT_REVIEW.md`; Guzman et al. 2016 report CA3-CA3 connections of one or two contacts, single-contact connections in the model). Their model still completes patterns, with about 1% connectivity but real CA3 size (thousands of inputs per cell, press release; UNVERIFIED in full). So "few multi-synapse pairs" is biological; what is non-biological is **our memory rule that every cue pair needs at least 5 synapses and only 4 cue cells exist.** That is a design choice about code size (4 cue cells out of 96), not a growth defect.
2. **Hubs and heavy-tailed in-degree are a growth feature** (0-2 cells with in-degree up to 95, 4-13 target cells with 6-30 times the median input). Here the literature has tools (degree-controlled graphs, in-degree normalisation, touch pruning), and also warnings that heterogeneous input wiring can break E/I balance (Landau 2016) and that hubs do exist in developing hippocampal GABA networks (Bonifazi 2009), so hubs are not automatically wrong, but they need a rule.
Items below say which one they attack.

## 7. Ranking: top 5 for us, and what each attacks
1. **Test whether the bottleneck is the design rule: distributed-cue assemblies (Guzman 2016, Treves-Rolls, Willshaw regime).** Compute, on the 10 E4 graphs, the number of assemblies with cue sizes 8-20 where each target needs about 17 single-synapse inputs, instead of 4 cue cells with at least 5 synapses each. This tests the claim of section 0 (item 1). **Attacks the design rule, not the grown graph**; it is also the only item whose model is documented to work with single-contact connections. Offline, about a day.
2. **Degree-controlled null models (configuration model, Erdos-Renyi) on the same graphs, with the same packer.** Compare capacity on the grown graph, on a degree-preserving randomisation (Molloy-Reed, Newman-Strogatz-Watts) and on Erdos-Renyi of the same p. **Attacks hubs directly** (it tells whether hubs help, hurt, or are irrelevant). Offline, hours; reuses `lpw_lib.py`.
3. **Touch-to-connectome pruning with in-degree and synapses-per-pair constraints (Reimann 2015, Markram 2015, Hjorth 2020).** From the E4 synapse lists compute what a pruned connectome would look like (in-degree capped, pair synapse counts matched to the literature) and recount candidate targets. **Attacks grown connectivity and hubs.** Offline for the recount; applying it needs a way to zero or delete synapses in NES (Randal said weight 0 is possible; the API sets pair conductances).
4. **Capacity scaling baseline (Treves-Rolls 1991, Roudi-Latham 2007, Derrida-Gardner-Zippelius 1987).** With in-degree C about 27 the theory predicts a handful of memories; compute the C (and the N at p = 0.28) needed for 10 or 20 memories. **Attacks scale**, not hubs; also tells whether more cells at the same p is the real remedy. Offline, minutes.
5. **Fit the growth parameters to bottleneck statistics with a noise-aware optimiser (ABC / SNPE / constrained noisy Bayesian optimisation: Beaumont 2002, Lueckmann 2017, Letham 2019).** Objective: in-degree CV, share of pairs with at least 3 synapses, candidate target count; constraints: the 7 bars. **Attacks grown connectivity at the source**, but only after items 1-3 tell us which statistic to optimise; costs new growths (batches of 3).
Items 1 and 4 are the cheapest and settle whether further work on growth is worth it; items 2 and 3 settle whether the hubs are the problem.
